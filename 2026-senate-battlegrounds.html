Polls are not predictions. An opinion poll estimates voter preferences among its respondents. A forecast evaluates how a race might unfold, using evidence and judgment. Neither a poll lead nor a model's probability guarantees an election outcome. Will's calls are independent assessments, not official results or a claim of statistical certainty.

Maine · U.S. Senate

Susan Collins vs. Troy Jackson

Republican incumbent Susan Collins faces Democrat Troy Jackson. The contest is especially important because Collins has a long record of winning in a state that has often voted Democratic in presidential elections. The campaigns have debated political independence, affordability and reproductive rights. A recent Washington Post–Schar School poll gives Jackson a narrow advantage, but the result falls within its margin of error.

Reporting: Associated Press ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

The central contradiction

Maine's presidential voting record and Susan Collins's Senate victories tell different stories. The state has regularly supported Democratic presidential nominees while returning Collins, a Republican, to Washington. That history is a warning against treating the presidential margin as an automatic Senate forecast. Voters may evaluate an incumbent differently from a national party, especially when the incumbent has cultivated a separate political identity over several elections.

What the polls actually establish

An early-October Marist survey found Troy Jackson at 51% and Collins at 47% among registered voters. That is a four-point measured lead in that sample, not a four-point guarantee on Election Day. Surveys of likely voters, different field dates and different undecided-voter treatments can produce different margins. The most useful question is whether a change appears across several independent pollsters rather than in just one headline result.

The real campaign argument

Jackson has questioned how independent Collins is from national Republican leadership. Collins points to her record of cross-party decisions and her ability to deliver resources for Maine. Their debates also cover the cost of living, trade with Canada, federal spending and reproductive rights. Those issues are relevant to the forecast only insofar as they change who votes or how voters assess the candidates; a memorable debate moment is not a measured shift in support.

How to read Will's forecast

The published rating on Will's interactive Senate map should be read alongside two competing pieces of evidence: Jackson's measured support in recent surveys and Collins's record of winning votes that presidential Republicans have not always received. If the forecast differs from a raw polling margin, the methodological question is how much weight to assign to previous Maine polling errors and incumbency. No particular size or direction of a future polling error is guaranteed.

What could change the assessment

Look for late surveys conducted after the debates, especially those with transparent likely-voter screens and clearly reported undecided shares. It also matters whether Jackson's support persists among independents, whether Collins's crossover voters remain with her, and whether a shift appears in multiple polls. A small movement in one sample should receive less weight than a repeated trend supported by different methods.

Texas · U.S. Senate

Ken Paxton vs. James Talarico

Republican Ken Paxton and Democrat James Talarico are contesting a seat in a state that has not elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate in decades. Several late-September and early-October surveys show a close contest, with results varying by pollster. Texas is a useful test of how candidate dynamics can interact with a state's usual partisan lean.

Polling reference: University of Texas ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

The partisan baseline

Texas has a long record of Republican statewide victories. That history matters because an unusually close Senate poll has to be understood against the much broader electorate that has voted Republican in recent elections. At the same time, a state's historical party alignment is not a fixed percentage: different candidates, issues and turnout levels can produce a result that differs from a past presidential race.

Why the surveys look so competitive

The University of Texas/Texas Politics Project survey reported James Talarico at 50% and Ken Paxton at 47%, while an October InsiderAdvantage poll listed Paxton at 49% and Talarico at 48%. Those findings are not identical, and their differences matter. Compare sampling populations, field dates and methodology before describing the trend. A two- or three-point margin can easily be smaller than the combined uncertainty surrounding an election estimate.

Candidate differences and campaign resources

Talarico and Paxton have distinct public records, campaign messages and fundraising operations. Their campaigns are fighting over economic conditions, national political identity and the candidates' personal and governing records. Heavy advertising is evidence that campaigns are investing in the race; it is not direct evidence that an advertisement has changed someone's vote. Suburban participation and whether occasional voters turn out are also important analytical questions.

Why a forecast is not just the average

Will's published prediction may place different weight on current candidate-specific polling and Texas's longer Republican voting history. A well-explained rating should make that tension explicit instead of treating either the historical margin or today's poll as decisive. For example, a narrow polling advantage can coexist with uncertainty about which voters will actually cast ballots. Any numerical probability on the live site is a forecast assumption, not a number measured by a survey.

What to watch next

Track whether Talarico and Paxton move in the same direction in several polls taken over comparable dates; monitor the share undecided, turnout screening and how results vary by pollster. Separate a genuine multi-poll trend from random sampling variation. An additional poll with a larger sample helps only if its design, transparency and voter population are appropriate.

Ohio · U.S. Senate special election

Jon Husted vs. Sherrod Brown

Republican Senator Jon Husted faces former Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown in a state where both parties see an opportunity. Economics, energy costs and the role of new data centers have become campaign issues. Ohio illustrates why analysts should compare candidate-specific polling with recent statewide voting patterns, not rely on one measure alone.

Reporting: Associated Press ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

Why this is an unusual rematch

The Ohio contest is a Senate special election between appointed Republican incumbent Jon Husted and former Democratic senator Sherrod Brown. Brown's previous statewide victories demonstrate that voters have elected him even as the state has shifted toward Republicans in presidential elections; his 2024 defeat also shows the limits of relying on an older personal vote. Both pieces of history matter, but neither decides the present race.

The difference between preference and expectation

Bowling Green State University's October polling put Brown at 48% and Husted at 42% in the Senate preference question. In the same research, respondents were evenly divided, 48% to 48%, on who they expected to win. This illustrates why perceived electability, personal voting preference and forecast probability are three different things. Neither question is an official result, and both are subject to survey limitations.

Issues and spending

Ohio's campaign has focused partly on industrial and energy policy, household costs, and the growth of electricity-hungry data centers. Campaign and outside-group spending is substantial. Large spending totals indicate the intensity of the contest, but dollars spent cannot simply be converted into votes gained. Analysts should examine whether a candidate's support changes after major advertising and whether the movement is corroborated across surveys.

Putting the race rating in context

A forecast can treat Brown's personal electoral history as meaningful while still accounting for Ohio's more recent Republican statewide baseline and Husted's incumbency. The central question is how much the Senate electorate will resemble the presidential electorate. This is not answered by the presidential margin alone, a single poll, or the candidates' fundraising. The rating above reflects Will's existing site data, not a new prediction supplied by this article.

What could move the rating

Compare later polls from different organizations, ideally distinguishing registered and likely voters. Watch whether undecided voters consolidate and whether the gap between vote preference and expected winner narrows. If polling shifts toward one candidate consistently across methods, that is stronger evidence than one unusually large lead in a survey with a broad margin of error.

Alaska · U.S. Senate

Dan Sullivan vs. Mary Peltola

Incumbent Republican Dan Sullivan faces Democrat Mary Peltola, a former member of the U.S. House. The race has become unusually competitive for Alaska, with energy policy and affordability among the subjects receiving attention. Alaska's distinctive political environment makes national polling trends an incomplete guide to the outcome.

Reporting: Reuters ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

Start with Alaska's unusual election system

Alaska's 2026 general election uses ranked-choice voting. Voters may rank candidates, and if no one wins a majority of first-choice votes, elimination rounds transfer ballots according to the next available preference. That means a two-candidate head-to-head question, a first-choice survey and a modeled final round are not interchangeable. A forecast that ignores the distinction can be misleading even if all the survey arithmetic is correct.

Why the candidates have different strengths

Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan brings a record of Senate service, while Democrat Mary Peltola has already campaigned successfully statewide for the House. Alaska also has a distinctive combination of rural communities, Native voters, military interests and voters who do not always align neatly with either national party. The size and composition of the actual electorate are important, particularly when relatively small survey samples try to represent widely dispersed communities.

What campaign issues may measure

Sullivan and Peltola have debated energy development, high prices, fisheries policy, healthcare and America's role in international conflicts. Each subject may influence voters differently across Alaska. Coverage of a debate can describe the candidates' positions, but it cannot by itself tell us how many people changed their minds. Analysts need survey evidence and turnout information before drawing a numerical conclusion.

Why a first-round lead may not settle it

A poll might show one candidate ahead on first preferences while leaving substantial support for other listed candidates. Under ranked-choice tabulation, later preferences from supporters of eliminated candidates can affect the final margin. Different surveys may ask different questions about rankings, and many do not have enough information to estimate transfers credibly. Will's published race rating should be considered in light of that specific uncertainty.

What to watch

Read Alaska polls carefully: are they first-choice results, hypothetical two-way matchups or full ranked-choice simulations? Monitor how many voters support candidates other than Sullivan and Peltola and how many have no later preferences. Updated sample sizes, field dates and demonstrated transfer assumptions are more helpful than presenting all poll numbers as though they represented the same measurement.

Iowa · U.S. Senate

Ashley Hinson vs. Josh Turek

Republican Representative Ashley Hinson and Democratic state Representative Josh Turek are competing for an open Senate seat. The candidates have debated tariffs, economic pressures and claims about their records. Iowa offers a test of whether a close candidate-specific race can develop in a state that has generally favored Republicans in recent statewide elections.

Reporting: The Washington Post ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

The baseline versus an open seat

Iowa has voted Republican in recent presidential elections, but this year's Senate contest does not feature the incumbent senator seeking another term. Open seats remove part of the personal incumbency advantage and can create an electorate that evaluates two candidates without the same history of voting for either in that office. It is still important not to overstate what that change means: the state's existing party preferences remain a separate piece of evidence.

A clear example of sample differences

Marist's October 8–11 poll found Democrat Josh Turek at 48% and Republican Ashley Hinson at 45% among likely voters, with a reported margin of sampling error of plus or minus 6.6 percentage points. The same poll found a larger Turek margin among registered voters. Those are different populations. A registered-voter sample includes people who may not vote, while a likely-voter screen tries to anticipate actual participation. Neither approach is infallible.

What else the Marist survey reveals

Marist also reported that many respondents expected Hinson to win despite reporting a close race in their personal preferences. That contrast can reflect perceptions of Iowa's underlying partisanship, not necessarily an actual future vote. The campaign includes debates over tariffs and economic pressures. To understand those issues empirically, separate what the candidates emphasize from what representative voters say matters most.

The forecasting challenge

A close Senate survey in a state with a Republican presidential baseline creates two competing signals. A thoughtful explanation of Will's rating should say whether the live model weights the latest candidate-specific data heavily or deliberately leaves room for turnout and historical error. One poll near the midpoint of its margin of error is not enough to prove a lasting change in the state's electorate.

What could change the forecast

Look for new Iowa likely-voter polls after the Marist field dates, especially if they disclose sample construction and how undecided voters are treated. Compare independent pollsters instead of treating every poll as equally informative. A persistent multi-poll trend, or meaningful evidence about actual voter participation, is more useful than a single survey swing.

North Carolina · U.S. Senate

Roy Cooper vs. Michael Whatley

Former Democratic Governor Roy Cooper is running against Republican Michael Whatley. North Carolina is a frequent battleground, but the candidates have different levels of statewide electoral experience. Late-campaign spending decisions and turnout could matter as much as the latest headline poll. Follow the live forecast for the current race rating rather than treating this overview as a prediction.

Reporting: Axios Raleigh ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

Why the candidates bring different statewide histories

North Carolina has been closely contested in national elections while also electing statewide candidates from both major parties to some offices. Former Democratic governor Roy Cooper has a record of winning statewide; Republican Michael Whatley has a different political background. A Senate contest is not a replay of a governor's race, but Cooper's previous campaigns provide evidence worth considering when interpreting his current polling.

How to interpret the larger polling margins

An October SurveyUSA survey published a 54% to 40% Cooper–Whatley result among likely voters. Other polls have produced different margins. A double-digit lead in a survey is a fact about the respondents and the poll's assumptions, not a certified vote margin or a precise probability. Poll aggregation can help reveal whether the result is an outlier, but the average depends on which polls qualify and how they are weighted.

Turnout matters even with a substantial polling lead

North Carolina's electorate contains rapidly growing communities, long-standing regional differences and voters with varying party loyalty. Early-voting activity may reveal participation, but it does not automatically show which candidate is benefiting. Vote-method differences, age composition and likely-voter assumptions can matter more to the final estimate than a single national political headline.

The forecast-versus-poll question

If Will's site gives the race a rating that appears more cautious than the latest poll margin, the key distinction is between a measured lead and uncertainty around a future electorate. Incumbency is not the central factor here because the seat is open; candidate familiarity, turnout and the consistency of separate polls may matter more. The website should explain those factors without implying it has measured effects that the data do not support.

What to watch next

Check whether Cooper's measured advantage is consistent across multiple likely-voter pollsters and whether undecided voters and sample composition shift. Be careful with comparisons between a poll of all registered voters and one of likely voters. The strongest case for changing a forecast is a pattern supported by updated, comparable evidence, not one surprising number.

Georgia · U.S. Senate

Jon Ossoff vs. Mike Collins

Democratic incumbent Jon Ossoff and Republican Representative Mike Collins have clashed over tariffs, the economy and their governing records. Georgia is important not only for the national Senate balance but also for what its results could reveal about turnout and the durability of recent voting coalitions.

Reporting: Georgia Public Broadcasting ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

How Georgia became a closely watched Senate state

Georgia's recent statewide races have featured very tight margins and substantial variation in turnout across metro Atlanta, its suburbs and the rest of the state. Jon Ossoff is the sitting Democratic senator, while Republican Mike Collins is a member of the U.S. House. Incumbency and name recognition matter as context, but neither should be treated as a substitute for evidence about this particular election.

What recent polling can and cannot say

Recent public polls have reported an Ossoff lead, although individual polls have different field dates, samples and margins. A poll average is useful for summarizing a set of measurements, but even a consistent lead cannot guarantee the distribution of votes on Election Day. Analysts should distinguish whether a poll asks registered or likely voters and whether it includes all options voters will see on the ballot.

The campaigns' substantive disagreements

In their October debates, Ossoff and Collins presented different accounts of tariffs and their effects on affordability and manufacturing. They also disagreed over federal healthcare policy and the war in Iran. Reporting on those positions helps explain the policy stakes, but whether they change voting behavior is an empirical question. A forecast should focus on how those disputes relate to actual surveys, past turnout and undecided voters.

Why statewide margins deserve caution

Georgia's electorate is not uniform. Small shifts in a few large communities, or turnout differences between presidential and midterm years, can change a close statewide result without a broad change in every county. Will's rating above is his published call. A defensible explanation should identify the limits of transferring a past presidential result into a Senate forecast, and avoid interpreting fundraising as votes.

What could change the assessment

Watch polling after the debates, including how large the undecided share remains. Compare surveys from different organizations and their sampling populations. Actual early-voting totals may provide context about turnout, but they do not reveal voters' choices by themselves. If evidence moves in a consistent direction across sources, the rationale for updating the published rating becomes stronger.

Michigan · U.S. Senate

Abdul El-Sayed vs. Mike Rogers

Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers are competing in another closely followed open-seat contest. Their debates have emphasized economic pressures, foreign policy and contrasting political records. Michigan offers a different electoral test from the Republican-held seats Democrats are trying to flip elsewhere.

Reporting: The Guardian ↗
Read the deeper race analysis

What an open seat changes

The retirement of Democratic senator Gary Peters makes Michigan's 2026 Senate race an open-seat election between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers. With no Senate incumbent defending the seat, the candidates' own histories, campaign organizations and messages may matter more than an incumbent's established constituent-service record. But the state's close recent presidential results remain important context for judging unusually large or small survey margins.

The polling signal and its limitations

Recent Michigan surveys have put El-Sayed and Rogers only a few points apart. For example, an October Rasmussen survey reported El-Sayed at 46% and Rogers at 44%; different pollsters have found different margins. A narrow lead is useful evidence about the sample and field period, not a prediction that it will persist. Comparing candidate preferences with the voting population modeled by each poll is essential.

Why the issues are locally distinctive

The campaigns have addressed economic conditions, employment in Michigan's automotive industry, foreign policy and their political records. Rogers previously served in Congress, while El-Sayed has a public-health and policy background. Those differences help explain the contest's lines of argument, but no analyst can reliably convert a candidate biography or a debate sound bite directly into a percentage of the final vote.

A useful caution from Michigan's primary

Michigan's Democratic primary illustrated how polling can vary significantly from a final close result. That does not prove that general-election polling will miss in the same direction or by the same amount: primary and general electorates differ. It does show why Will's explanation should separate polling averages, candidate history and assumptions about which groups will actually turn out.

What would justify a model update

Track multiple late likely-voter surveys, their field dates, sample types and undecided shares. Treat reported advertising expenditures as campaign context, not an automatic change in vote margin. A clear, sustained movement across independent polls is more persuasive than any single result. The most transparent forecast records when its rating changed and which new evidence prompted the change.
