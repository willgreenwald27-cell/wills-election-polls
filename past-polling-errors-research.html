What is a polling error?

Polling error is the difference between a poll estimate and the election outcome, often described in percentage points or in the margin between candidates. Polls may differ from the eventual result because of sampling uncertainty, turnout, late changes in opinion, measurement issues, or errors shared across multiple surveys.

Errors can move in different directions across states, parties, and election cycles. Comparing the final result with polling averages is a useful first step, but understanding when the surveys were fielded is also important.

Explore the historical comparisons

Visit the site's interactive Polls vs. Reality page for its historical polling-error visualizations and election comparisons. The collection covers presidential-election cycles from 2000 through 2024. The goal is to put polling estimates and actual electoral outcomes side by side so readers can examine where expectations held and where they missed.

Why polling misses matter for 2026

For definitions of polling averages, sampling uncertainty and race-rating terminology, see how to read Senate forecasts and polls.

When a 2026 Senate contest appears close, even a modest miss in a polling average could change which candidate finishes ahead. The history of polling error is a reminder to avoid treating narrow leads as certainties. It can also help readers ask better questions about sample composition, undecided voters, and the relationship between polling and turnout.

Past misses should not be applied mechanically to the current election. Election conditions and survey methods differ across years, and a pattern in one state does not prove it will recur in another.
